Why it was declined

It flips the one distinction board ① is built on. An inventory counts the house’s work; the moment the same line counts the reader’s, the hub becomes a scorecard you meet before you have chosen anything. E4’s refusal column names completion percentages and the endowed-progress effect outright, and Nunes & Drèze is the reason: a part-filled bar is not information, it is a pull.

It also fails the metric test on its own terms. A “halls visited” figure has exactly one success condition — the number going up — and the bus is unambiguous: “a pattern fails if its success metric is retention.” There is no version of this that is not a retention instrument wearing a helpful face.

And it would have needed the realm to watch a vessel walk. The bus forbids walk-state as analytics and names surveillance a sensory harm in its own right — “no accessibility standard names freedom-from-observation as an access need. The house does.” The Honors half is worse still: it is the unfiltered catalog KP ruled out on board ⑥, redrawn as a bar.

What was kept from it

The instinct underneath was right: a door should say what is behind it. That survives as board ①’s six inventory lines — six quests on the shelf, one course · six steps, thirty stars · five collections — which answer the same question honestly, and would read exactly the same on a vessel’s first visit as on their thousandth.
