Six equal tiles, three across, as the room stands today.

The case for it, honestly

It is what is built, it costs nothing, and the two extra tiles are real rooms a vessel may want. Removing a door from a hub is the kind of tidying that looks like taste and lands like loss — and the six-tile grid is genuinely tidier at three across than four are at two.

The ground that declined it

“a bazaar experience that is not overwhelming or time consuming”

KP ⚛, THE IMMERSION VISION, verbatim · E2 bus L606–607; read on the same bus as “in-and-out with dignity… a REFUSAL of the mall-model” (L637–641).

A mall directory lists every unit in the building, because the building’s purpose is that you walk past all of them. A bazaar entrance shows you the stalls. Six tiles is not overwhelming by itself — but two of the six are not stalls at all, and putting them on the same grid at the same weight teaches the eye that this is an inventory of a building rather than a way in.

The Exchange tile is the sharper half. The room itself says CheckoutHub.tsx:91: “The Exchange completes on each work’s page.” A door to a room that tells you to go somewhere else is a corridor, and a corridor placed at the entrance is the Gruen transfer’s smallest brick.

What was kept from it

Both rooms survive, and neither loses a visitor. Contributions stands as one quiet line below the grid — still one click, no longer a stall. The Exchange keeps its route and gains a better inbound: a link from the stall, beside the split, where a vessel actually wants to know how an exchange works. It is reached at the moment it means something instead of at the moment it means nothing.

And the refusal produced a finding it would not otherwise have surfaced: taking the tile away exposed that /bazaar/checkout has exactly one inbound link in the whole realm. That is on board ⑦ now, with the mend.
